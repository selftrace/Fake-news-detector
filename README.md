# Fake-news-detector
Fake news detection using natural language processing

This one started because I wanted to mess around with NLP a bit more. Up until now I'd mostly been working with numerical datasets where every feature already existed and all I really had to think about was the algorithm itself. Text is a completely different problem. A machine learning model can't exactly look at a sentence and understand English, so the first challenge was figuring out how to turn words into something the model could actually work with.

The dataset itself is pretty straightforward. It contains articles labelled as either fake or real, which makes it another binary classification problem. Before training anything though, I had to clean the text a bit. Everything gets converted to lowercase, punctuation, URLs and numbers get stripped out, and generally the articles are reduced down into something a little more consistent. It isn't exactly sophisticated preprocessing, but it's enough to remove a lot of noise without completely destroying the information in the text.

The part I found most interesting wasn't actually the classifiers themselves, it was TF-IDF. I'd heard people mention it constantly but never really looked into what it was doing. Turns out it's a pretty clever way of representing text. Instead of just counting how many times a word appears, it also considers how common that word is across every document. Words that appear everywhere become less important while words that are more unique to particular articles get larger weights. It feels a lot more sensible than treating every word equally.

Once everything was vectorised I ended up trying several different classifiers instead of stopping after the first one. Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest all got thrown at exactly the same TF-IDF vectors. The nice thing about sklearn is that once the preprocessing is done, swapping algorithms is almost embarrassingly easy. You fit one model, change a couple lines, fit another, compare the results and see what happens.

One thing that surprised me was how well some of the models performed. At first I was sitting there thinking I'd accidentally become brilliant overnight, then I remembered machine learning usually isn't that generous. More likely the dataset itself makes the task easier than it appears. Articles from different sources often have noticeably different writing styles, vocabulary and formatting. So instead of actually deciding whether a claim is true or false, the model might just be learning statistical patterns in how fake and real articles tend to be written. That's a pretty important distinction.

That was probably the biggest takeaway from this project. Calling it "fake news detection" is a bit misleading because the model isn't fact-checking anything. It has absolutely no idea whether an article is objectively true. All it's doing is looking at the words, comparing them to patterns it saw during training, and making its best guess based on those statistics. If you gave it a completely fabricated article written in the style of a reputable news outlet, there's a decent chance it'd happily call it real.

I also added a simple manual testing function at the end because I wanted to see what happened when I pasted in my own text. There's something a lot more satisfying about typing in a random headline and watching four different models argue over it than just looking at an accuracy score.

Overall this project ended up being less about building another classifier and more about understanding how traditional NLP pipelines actually work. It was my first proper introduction to TF-IDF, text preprocessing and classical text classification, and it made me appreciate how much of machine learning isn't really about the model at all. Sometimes the hardest part is just figuring out how to represent the data in a way the model can understand.
